\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The Taxonomy Predicts the Variance, Not the Sign}

Narrowing components, which can only remove or re-weight decisions the rule already makes, produced the smallest and
most consistently positive effects in both studies, and in Study~2 not a single significantly negative one. Replacing
components produced the largest effects in both directions. The ex-ante hypothesis of Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy} is
therefore supported. What the taxonomy does not predict is \emph{which} direction a replacing component takes; that
depends on how well the replaced mechanism fits the strategy. A learned continuation exit is redundant for
trend-followers, whose trailing stops already let winners run, but it repairs a genuine weakness of a mean-reversion
rule whose fixed profit target truncates them. Symmetrically, a learned entry that finds trends is a poor substitute
for a rule designed to fade them. MBCA's practical output is therefore a question to ask of each insertion point:
\emph{does the learned model address a known weakness of the rule it replaces?}

\subsection{What Study~1 Got Right and Wrong}

Study~2 confirms Study~1's central conclusion---no component reliably and significantly improves a trend-following
control at this budget---and its taxonomy-level reading. It revises three specifics. ML exit is not intrinsically
harmful: its Study~1 losses were inflated by optimistic stop fills and are specific to trend-following rules. The
meta-label + sizing pairing, pre-registered in Study~2 as an out-of-sample test of a post hoc choice, is positive in
most cells but significant in only \STwoMktBestGain{} of \STwoMktBestN{}. And the forex-specific claims of Study~1 do
not survive a change of data vendor.

\subsection{Why the Effects Are Small}

The controls themselves have little out-of-sample edge (Fig.~\ref{fig:s2decay}), and a matched budget of eight generic
price features gives a learned component little information that the rule does not already use. Where the rule has a
structural weakness, such as the RSI(2) exit, ML finds it; where it does not, the best a narrowing component can do is
trade a little less. This is consistent with the data-snooping literature \cite{sullivan1999data,park2007technical}:
once rules are fixed in advance and evaluated under realistic costs, the room for improvement is small.

\subsection{Practical Implications}

Fig.~\ref{fig:decision} condenses the evidence into a decision guide for a practitioner with a rule-based system.
Prefer narrowing components, and expect them to reduce turnover and exposure more than to raise the Sharpe ratio. Use
a replacing component only where the replaced rule has an identifiable weakness, and test it under realistic fills and
costs. Evaluate every variant with conservative execution before attributing anything to ML, because execution
assumptions move the Sharpe ratio more than any component. Finally, require replication at a second, independent
freeze date before acting on a single-window improvement.
